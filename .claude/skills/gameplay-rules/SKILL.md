# Gameplay rules

These are what the game promises, held up by `tests/gameplay-rules.test.js` (one `test()` per id, on the live
`CITIES`, `NETS`, `CABLES`, `SCN`, `PZ`, `PRESETS`…). Each rule is true of the intended design.

## Procedure

1. Changing anything above? Run `npm test -- tests/gameplay-rules.test.js` (under a minute) before committing.
2. A failure names what breaks the rule and how to fix it. Fix the data or the code.
3. Only when real life really is different, add an entry to `EXCEPTIONS` in the test (keyed by rule id, sorted, with
   its reason in plain words). Never delete or loosen a check to make it pass. A stale exception fails too.
4. A new rule gets an id in the right section here, one line, a why, and its own `test('ID: …')`.

## World

| Id | Rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| W1 | Every city has a country code (`cc`), a region (an Australian state or territory, or `int` for the gateways overseas), a landmass and coordinates; a POI city also carries its premises. | Prices, eyeballs and progression hang off the state. |
| W2 | Every state and territory has at least two retail networks (eyeball networks, or Tier 2s that sell to homes) with a PoP there. | Real states have a choice of RSPs; a state with one network has no peering to win. |
| W3 | Transit is sold in every Australian data centre (`isDc`: the capitals and the regional colocation cities), by at least two carriers, and nowhere else: not at a POI, a route town or an overseas PoP (those are for peering). Every data centre is a starting city, and nothing else is (weekly scenarios included). Caches, PNIs and exchange ports are bought only in a data centre or an overseas hub (`inDc`), rack space only in an Australian data centre. Cables stop at the landing stations overseas (`ls`), which sell nothing. | A start or an expansion must never be a dead end by accident. |
| W4 | Cable pairs join two distinct real cities, have a length, and cable ids are unique. | Broken landings break routing and the own-cable planner. |
| W5 | A city alone on its landmass has a cable or a beach to land one. | Islands are reachable. |
| W6 | All 121 NBN points of interconnect are cities, each with its premises and state, and every state and territory has at least one. Generated by `scripts/build-au-map.js`. | The NBN is the game's map of where homes are. |

## Networks

| Id | Rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N1 | Network ids and ASNs are unique; ASNs are public (the real one for real-world networks). | Saves key on ids; players look ASNs up. |
| N2 | Names are puns: no network, cable, exchange, data center, router or optic is called by a real name (blocklist `REAL_NAMES` in the test), and no lazy "-ish" names. | CLAUDE.md. Add the real name to the blocklist whenever you add its pun. |
| N3 | Every non-Tier-1, non-content network is in a founding Tier 1's or a big Australian carrier's (Telstraya, Floptus, Toilet Paper Group, Hocus Pocus) customer cone (`up`), or is a documented island (transit-free incumbents, China, Korea, Australia…). Upstreams exist and are never content. | Tier 1 by customer cone (#85): a new network outside the cones makes Tier 1 harder for every save, old ones included. |
| N4 | No upstream cycles. | Cones must be trees, not loops. |
| N5 | Traffic shares are positive and below 20%; `NSH` sums to 1; content carries at least 30%. | Shares drive every flow; eyeballs mostly ask for traffic. |
| N6 | Every network has a type, a peering policy (selective ones state `minIx`/`minPni`), a colour and a translated blurb; carriers have a price. | Panels and the peering rules read them. |
| N7 | PoPs are real, distinct cities; exchange ports only where the city has an exchange and the network has a PoP. | Presence follows PeeringDB-style reality. |

## Modes and rules

| Id | Rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M1 | On-call is the classic game: it equals `RULE_DEF`, and a save without rules loads as On-call. The seeded suites (`tests/game.test.js`, `tests/rules.test.js`) are its behaviour. | Old saves never change under them. |
| M2 | Presets are ordered: Change Freeze never harsher than On-call, BGP at 3 a.m. never gentler (chaos, lead times, market; and a seeded year of incidents). | The names promise it. |
| M3 | Every subsystem that can annoy the player reads a rule: outages and events (`chaos`), DDoS, sold-out routes, de-peering and hardware prices (`market`), lead times (`lead`). A quiet ruleset gives a quiet year. | Rules are presets of flags, not separate code. |
| M5 | Sandbox has no locked feature: open gates, every level, own cables, endless money. | It is a sandbox. |

## Fun and fairness

| Id | Rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | A new network gets a gentle start: no outage before day 45, no event before day 40 and only good news before day 50, nothing sold out for 90 days, hardware prices hold for 180, and a new customer loses no mood for 15 days. | New players learn before they get punished. |
| F2 | On On-call, a simple build in a start city (broadband, the cheapest 100G transit, an exchange port with the route server) makes money within 3 months, in Sydney, Melbourne, Perth and Brisbane. | Seeded today it turns a profit in month 1 or 2; a balance change that breaks this makes the start too hard. |
| F3 | A specialist path (#187) only brings a customer type in below its level when the network can already serve it, or nearly: the spotted cities have POPs, the latency is met, the fiber carries at least three quarters of the smallest order. | Capability, not luck: an offer you cannot fulfil with a reasonable build is a trap. |
| F4 | The chaos monkey visits only On-call and BGP at 3 a.m. (never Change Freeze or Sandbox), not before day 180, for 120 to 240 days; while it is here its snips replace the ordinary failures, each snip heals within a week, and it goes after tubes with no other way round first. | A test of resilience you can prepare for, not a pile-on: the reward goes to networks built with a second path. |
| F5 | Capacity you rent to other carriers never outgrows the route's market: a broker listing sells over time as carriers between the two cities want it, and neither it nor a random offer sells past what both cities' eyeballs support (a landing town counts the big city it serves). | Rent that scales with your own glass and not with demand is a money machine (#203). |

## DDoS

| Id | Rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | Attack sizes come from the world, never from your network: a heavy tail (median near 9 G, one in a hundred over 650 G) scaled by the era, capped at the era's record, and for the first 180 days (or with one POP) never more than twice your biggest port. Ten times the subscribers means the same attacks. | Sizes that grew with the network made every late-game flood larger than the internet's record (#242). |
| D2 | A flood enters on every port the internet reaches you on, by each network's share, and a port carries only what fits: everyone on a full port loses the same share. | Real floods are spread by geography; one fat port at a single POP is the weak spot (#242). |
| D3 | Only upstream tools (RTBH, clean pipe) and an engaged scrubbing center keep a flood off your ports. Flowspec acts on the ingress router, after the port, within that router's own rule budget. | Flowspec protects customers, not the external connections; TCAM is finite. |
| D4 | RTBH costs only the target: it goes dark (and its mood drops once), everyone else is saved, and the attack counts as sacrificed, not held. | RTBH is the tool that saves the network and is bad for that one customer, so it must be a real choice. |
